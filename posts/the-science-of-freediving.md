*A physiological journey into the deep.*

Mention diving and people think of oxygen tanks, decompression stops, a pile of technical gear. Freediving is the exact opposite: no tanks, no breathing underwater, so no decompression stops. Just you, your lungs, and the ocean. *But is it really that simple?*

Behind that apparent bareness sit physical and physiological mechanisms of striking elegance, and an evolutionary inheritance we share, to varying degrees, with the great champions of the deep: seals, sea lions, sperm whales and beaked whales. Let's dive in.

## Pressure, the first constraint

Underwater, pressure rises by one bar every 10 meters. By about 250 meters, sunlight has all but vanished. At nearly 11 kilometers, at the bottom of the Mariana Trench, pressure exceeds a thousand times that at the surface. James Cameron, one of the very few humans to have gone there in a submersible, in 2012, described the seafloor as "desolate, lunar-like": a gray plain, almost featureless, more alien than the surface of the Moon.<sup>[1](#n1)</sup>

More modestly, but with nothing but their lungs as an air supply, freedivers still reach vertiginous depths. The official record in the "no limit" discipline, where a weighted sled pulls the diver down and a balloon brings them back up, is 214 meters, set by the Austrian Herbert Nitsch in 2007. That discipline is now fading, judged too dangerous, in favor of "purer" approaches where the diver descends and ascends by muscle power alone. Which makes the question a fundamental one: where are the real limits of the human body?

## The diving reflex: a mammalian inheritance

The moment your face touches cold water, a remarkable mechanism kicks in: the **diving reflex**. Thermoreceptors in the skin of the face detect the sudden change in temperature and trigger a cascade of responses whose purpose is to save oxygen<sup>[2](#n2)</sup>:

- **Bradycardia**: the heart rate slows, sometimes drastically, to cut oxygen consumption.
- **Peripheral vasoconstriction**: the vessels of the limbs close down and blood is redirected to the vital organs: brain, heart, lungs.

The paradox is a nice one. At the surface, finning is physical effort and should speed the heart up. In a freediver, the opposite happens. This helping hand, inherited from our mammalian ancestors, switches on in the first seconds underwater without our doing anything at all.

## Going down: Boyle's law at work

As you descend, pressure makes itself felt on the body's air-filled cavities: lungs, ears, sinuses. **Boyle's law** is implacable: at constant temperature, pressure times volume stays constant. At 10 meters, pressure has doubled, so the volume of air in your lungs has halved. At 30 meters, it is a quarter.

For a long time physiologists believed there was an impassable limit around 40 meters. Beyond it, the compressed air would drop below the *residual volume*, the incompressible volume of the lungs that normally keeps their walls from collapsing, and the rib cage would cave in under the pressure.

Then Jacques Mayol and Enzo Maiorca, in the 1960s and 70s, went past it. The body revealed a countermeasure: the **blood shift**. Blood driven out of the periphery by vasoconstriction floods the pulmonary capillaries and fills the space left by the compressed air. Blood, unlike air, is incompressible: it holds the volume of the chest whatever the depth. The lung becomes, in a sense, a hydraulic shell.

## The tipping point: when the ocean pulls you in

Near the surface, your inflated lungs act as a float: you are buoyant. But as the air compresses, the volume shrinks, and with it the Archimedean upthrust. At a certain depth, which varies with body shape and weighting but is usually between 15 and 30 meters, buoyancy flips. You no longer need to fin. The ocean pulls you in.

This is one of the sensations freedivers seek most. All effort stops, you glide, drawn toward the deep. It is also the point at which oxygen economy is at its best, since the muscles have stopped working.

## Letting go, as a safety requirement

At depth, the instruction is paradoxical for an extreme sport: above all, do not resist. Muscular tension against the pressure can push plasma into the alveoli, an immersion pulmonary edema, a classic accident of deep freediving. The freediver learns to accept the compression, to relax, mentally and physically. Mental tension turns into physical tension, and the reverse.

This may be what makes freediving so singular as a discipline: it is probably the only extreme sport in which performance depends first on the ability to do nothing.

## The real champions: seals, sea lions, sperm whales

As impressive as human performance is, marine mammals crush us in every category. If you dive near Vancouver you may meet harbor seals or sea lions, sometimes a little too curious, like the sea lion that charged straight at me before veering off at the last moment. These animals routinely go below 300 meters. Their heart rate, around 100 beats per minute at the surface, can drop below 10 beats per minute on a dive: the same diving reflex as ours, taken to another level.

And then there is the deep. Sperm whales regularly go beyond 1,000 meters. But the absolute record among mammals belongs to **Cuvier's beaked whale** (*Ziphius cavirostris*): one documented dive to 2,992 meters, and another lasting 3 hours and 42 minutes, 222 minutes without breathing.<sup>[3](#n3)</sup>

How do they do it? Their lung size, relative to body mass, is nothing special. The key lies elsewhere: they store oxygen differently. Much better.

## The molecular secret: a non-stick myoglobin

**Myoglobin** is the protein that stores oxygen in muscle, the muscular counterpart of the hemoglobin in blood. The more of it you have, the larger the oxygen reserve you can draw on during effort. The muscles of the great divers are so saturated with it that they are almost black.

Except that at very high concentration, proteins run into a universal problem: they aggregate. They clump together and lose their function. How do sperm whales and beaked whales pack ten times more myoglobin into their muscles than we do without everything seizing up?

The answer came in 2013 from Michael Berenbrink's group at the University of Liverpool.<sup>[4](#n4)</sup> Comparing the myoglobin of about a hundred mammal species, from pig to water shrew by way of beaver, seal and sperm whale, they showed that the great divers have evolved a myoglobin with a **strongly positive net surface charge**. The molecules repel one another like the matching poles of small magnets. They can therefore be packed to colossal concentrations without ever aggregating.

A *non-stick* myoglobin, in short. Elegant. And by walking back up the evolutionary tree, the study even dated the appearance of this molecular signature in the aquatic ancestors of today's cetaceans, around fifty million years ago.

Add to that an extreme metabolic slowdown during the dive and a remarkable tolerance to CO₂ and muscle acidity, and the whole forms a physiological machine of remarkable coherence, tuned by tens of millions of years of selective pressure.

## One body, different limits

Freediving illustrates something simple: we carry the remnants of an aquatic evolutionary history. The diving reflex that slows our heart on contact with cold water is the same as the seal's. The blood shift that protects our lungs at 40 meters is the same mechanism that protects a sperm whale's at 2,000. The difference is not qualitative but quantitative. We have the building blocks. They have the whole architecture.

That may be what makes freediving so fascinating. Underwater, you are not fighting the body: you discover, with some surprise, that it already knows what to do. All it takes, in a sense, is letting it remember.
